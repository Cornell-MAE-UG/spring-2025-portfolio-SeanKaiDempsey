My project for Cornell Baja Racing 2025-2026 was to redesign the front and rear hubs for TG22. The hubs are critical components that transfer driveline torque to the ground while reacting all suspension loads and the braking forces in the front. The primary design challenge was handling complex combined loading while optimizing for weight savings. TG22 successfully competed with zero hub failures!

![Front Hub Cross Section]({{ "/assets/images/FrontHubCrossSection.png" | relative_url }}){: style="width: 75%;"}

## The Problem

TG22 saw a 15% increase in driveline torque over the previous car, requiring the hubs and inserts to withstand significantly higher loads. On top of that, the front hub's banana shape needed to get even more pronounced as the king pin offset (KPO) decreased from 2.1" to 2", pushing the hub-rim interface further inboard and creating tighter packaging constraints at the corner.

The legacy torque transfer insert design hadn't been updated since OD16 and used a simple square-with-rounded-corners profile which is not ideal for the new torque demands. Additionally, the spline insert was manufactured by purchasing OEM Yamaha hubs and machining out the original splines. This process proved costly, and the hubs were difficult to source.

## Hub Spline Insert Redesign

The biggest change for TG22 was eliminating the OEM Yamaha insert and designing a fully custom replacement. I reverse-engineered the OEM spline by referencing JIS standards and iterating the profile using 3D resin-printed test parts.

For the torque transfer profile I moved from the legacy square profile to a **p4c polygon**, designed using the team's torque transfer spreadsheet and validated in Ansys. After exploring a more rounded profile (FOS 1.6), I increased the eccentricity to better handle manufacturing variation and combined loading, landing on a final **FOS of 2.34**. I plotted the parametric profile in Desmos to visually verify the geometry, then confirmed manufacturability by ensuring the minimum radius of curvature stayed above 0.13", which is within the tolerance of the specified tooling.

![Front Hub Press Ansys]({{ "/assets/images/HubPressAnsys.png" | relative_url }}){: style="width: 720px; display: block; margin: 0 auto;"}


To integrate the insert into the hub body I used Ansys alongside legacy design benchmarks. The old profile had stress risers that hadn't caused failures in seven years, so I validated TG22's design by ensuring stresses were equal to or lower than the legacy hub. I improved the press FOS from 0.88 (TG21) to **1.37 (TG22)**.

## Front Hub

The front hubs retained and extended their banana shape to accommodate the reduced KPO. The tighter corner geometry created significant packaging challenges. Clearances became so tight that the OEM pressed-in studs had to be turned down by ~50 thou to fit within the wheel well. I updated the limiting load case to a combined curb slam with full driveline torque, then re-optimized the hub arms to pass FEA under this more demanding condition.

## Rear Hub

The rear hubs required less geometric change but still went through full re-analysis. Ansys FEA was used to optimize the design for weight under the updated load cases, confirming the rear hubs could handle increased torque and the new combined loading standard.

## FEA & Load Cases

![Front Hub Ansys]({{ "/assets/images/FrontHubKablamo.png" | relative_url }}){: .inline-image-r style="width: 360px"}


I significantly updated the hub load cases this year to capture realistic combined loading. The most demanding new case—**Press w/ Torque & Curb Slam +**—simulates the car landing on one tilted tire at full throttle, combining a 700 lb curb slam with full driveline shock torque and the non-linear press mechanics modeled as a multi-step solve in Ansys.

Other load cases included: press only, press with torque, shock torque, curb slam X, curb slam +, and braking forces on the front rotor towers. Past load cases were retained as comparison benchmarks since the team has had zero hub failures in prior years.

## Manufacturing

Both hub bodies were designed to be manufactured in-house on 3-axis Haas machines. The new insert was designed so that only the wire EDM operation needed to be outsourced.

Manufacturing wasn't without its challenges. A 60 thou gap discovered between the hub face and bearing required a post-op on the Haas to deepen the splines and add slight radial clearances. One insert was found to have a non-concentric spline profile which earned that hub the name "CHUB" and was set aside as a last-resort spare.
