One of the most interesting assignments for me was the photovoltaic (PV) panel thermal analysis. What drew me to this problem is how the the physical system needs to be modeled into a thermal resistance network. Rather than treating conduction, convection, radiation, and contact resistance separately, I had to model them as an integrated system with clearly defined temperature nodes and heat flux paths. Constructing the resistance network required me to carefully justify assumptions such as steady state, one-dimensional heat transfer, uniform heat generation in the silicon, constant material properties, and perfect insulation on the backside. Approximating radiation using an effective heat transfer coefficient and neglecting contact resistance between the glass and adhesive simplified the analysis while still capturing the dominant physics. This process strengthened my ability to reduce a complex real-world device into a solvable engineering model without losing physical meaning.

![PV panel schematic]({{ "/assets/images/PhotovoltaicPanel.png" | relative_url }}){: style="width: 80%;display: block; margin-left: auto; margin-right: auto;"}

A key insight from this assignment was understanding that the silicon temperature is not merely an output of the system, but a variable that directly influences how energy is partitioned within it. The solar-to-electricity conversion efficiency depended linearly on silicon temperature, meaning that as the silicon temperature increased, electrical efficiency decreased and a larger fraction of absorbed solar energy was converted into heat. However, because the system was analyzed at steady state, this relationship did not create an unstable feedback loop; instead, it modified the energy balance that determined the final equilibrium temperature. The silicon temperature can be solved directly by incorporating the temperature-dependent efficiency into the steady-state heat transfer model. This reinforced the importance of setting up a complete and consistent energy balance, where material behavior and performance characteristics are embedded within the governing equations rather than treated separately.


![PV panel thermal resistance network]({{ "/assets/images/ThermalResistanceDiagram.png" | relative_url }}){: style="width: 80%;display: block; margin-left: auto; margin-right: auto;"}


This assignment also highlighted important engineering tradeoffs. Higher silicon temperatures reduce electrical efficiency, so improving heat rejection through convection or radiation directly improves power output. At the same time, material choices, contact resistance, and layer thicknesses all influence thermal performance. Even small resistances, such as interfacial contact resistance, can meaningfully affect operating temperature in layered systems. The assumptions I made—steady state operation, 1D heat transfer, uniform heat generation, a very thin silicon layer, constant properties, approximated radiative transfer coefficient, no contact resistance between glass and adhesive, and perfect insulation at the back—were necessary to make the model tractable. However, reflecting on these assumptions helped me understand their physical implications and limitations. Overall, this problem strengthened my ability to model coupled energy systems, evaluate design tradeoffs, and critically assess the validity of simplifying assumptions—skills that will be essential in future work involving thermal and energy systems.
