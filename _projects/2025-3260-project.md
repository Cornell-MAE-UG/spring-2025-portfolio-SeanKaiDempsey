Rather than relying on a physical test bench, this project focused entirely on the mathematical modeling and control simulation of a thermal system. The objective was to design a PID controller capable of regulating a coffee warmer's temperature while minimizing steady-state error and settling time.

### Mathematical Modeling & ODE Formulation
I derived the governing differential equations for the system by establishing a transient thermal energy balance across the coffee and the heating plate assembly. The model couples the heat flux entering the fluid via conduction from the hot plate against the convective cooling losses escaped to the ambient environment:

$$C \frac{dT}{dt} = G_{bot} (T_{plt} - T) + G_{cup} (T_{air} - T)$$

Using MATLAB, my group set up and solved these non-linear ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to simulate the open-loop plant response and characterize the system's inherent thermal lag.

### Simulation-Driven PID Tuning
With the plant dynamics characterized, I implemented a feedback control loop. Because a physical prototype was not available, I used a systematic **trial-and-error** tuning within the simulation environment**:
- **Proportional Gain:** Increased to accelerate the initial rise time, balancing against system overshoot.
- **Integral Gain:** Introduced and tuned to eliminate the steady-state error inherent to the convective cooling losses.
- **Derivative Gain:** Adjusted cautiously to add damping and smooth out the transient approach to the target temperature.

### Results

Since this project was entirely simulation-based, the deliverable was the validated model and tuned controller rather than a physical measurement. The final PID controller achieved a stable closed-loop response that eliminated steady-state error and met the settling time target within the simulation. The proportional term drove fast initial response, the integral term eliminated the offset caused by convective losses, and the derivative term damped oscillations during the transient approach to setpoint.

The close agreement between the ODE-derived plant model and expected thermal behavior gave confidence that the controller would perform similarly on a real system. Although physical validation would be needed to confirm performance against real-world thermal lag and sensor noise.

This project reinforced how powerful simulation-driven design can be early in development: by fully characterizing the plant dynamics first, PID tuning became a methodical process rather than blind trial and error with a physical system.
